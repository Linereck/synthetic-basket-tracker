XRT as GME’s settlement shock absorber: findings, surprises, and the shape of a predictive model

What six years of SEC fails-to-deliver data, joined to Reg SHO threshold-list residencies, actually shows — what it proves, what it doesn’t, and which clocks are real enough to build a model on.

Data: SEC CNS fails-to-deliver 2019-01-02 → 2026-06-12 · NYSE/NASDAQ/CBOE threshold lists 2019-10-01 → 2026-07-02 · Interactive charts: the exhibits page

0Executive summary

  • XRT has spent 37% of all trading days since October 2019 on the Reg SHO threshold list — 633 of 1,697 days, across 15 residencies, 13 of them longer than the 13-day close-out ceiling the rule is built around. The longest ran 187 days (and is left-censored: it was already running when our data begins).
  • January 2021 is a handoff, visible in fail quantities. GME’s fails peak Jan 26; the buy button dies Jan 28; XRT posts its own fails peak and enters the threshold list Jan 29; GME’s fails collapse 94% within a week and it leaves the list Feb 3 — and has not appeared on any exchange’s threshold list since, through July 2026.
  • The biggest surprise: the close-out cliff is inverted. Fails are suppressed in the final days of each XRT residency — exactly what the 5-clean-day exit rule requires — then explode within five days of exiting, by up to ×85. Deliveries track the rule’s measurement windows, not economic settlement.
  • An honest null: a fixed-lag scan (25–45 calendar days) found no clean T+35 cycle in fail quantities. The timing structure is event-shaped (list entries, exits, fail clusters), not a standing calendar wave. This matters for how a predictive model should be built — and for how it shouldn’t.
  • 2024 rhymes but doesn’t repeat: GME’s May 2024 fail cluster precedes the price runs, but at one-quarter of 2021’s absolute size against a share count ~4× larger — which is arithmetically why GME never re-listed. The pressure now travels through channels the threshold list can’t see.

IFirst, the data had to be fixed

Every number in this report sits on a pipeline that was quietly wrong until this morning, and the corrections are large enough that they’re findings in their own right:

  • A stale holiday table was breaking every streak. The analysis hardcoded 2024–2025 market holidays only. Every 2026 holiday severed every streak: the morning report showed every security at “9 consecutive days” — IONZ’s true streak was 247. Holidays are now computed algorithmically for any year, and streak continuity is judged against each exchange’s observed publication calendar, which also absorbs one-off closures (the Carter funeral) automatically.
  • The CBOE downloader marked unpublished days as done forever. 115 weekdays sat checkpointed with zero rows; we proved recoverable data existed (2026-03-02 et al.) and backfilled it. The resume logic in all three downloaders could also permanently skip a date that failed while a later date succeeded — it ate CBOE’s 2026-07-02 the day before we caught it.
  • NYSE doesn’t publish threshold lists on Columbus Day or Veterans Day (equities trade; the bond market is closed). Treating those as “off the list” had split XRT’s longest streak into 9 + 178; per-exchange calendars merged it to the true 187.
  • The SEC fails dataset was 14 months stale — and the reason the “downloader failed” is that there never was one: the scripts only parsed ZIPs downloaded by hand, and sec.gov rejects clients without a descriptive User-Agent. A real downloader now exists; 1.53M rows were appended (append-only; the data repo’s git history is untouched).

Moral for the paper’s methods section: threshold-list analytics are extremely sensitive to calendar assumptions. Anyone replicating naïvely will understate every streak.

IIFindings

F1 — Residency, not visits

The threshold list is designed as a brief penalty box: five consecutive settlement days of aggregate fails ≥ 10,000 shares and ≥ 0.5% of shares outstanding puts you on; thirteen days on triggers mandatory close-out; five clean days take you off. XRT treats it as a residence: 97% of its 633 listed days sit inside residencies that outlasted the 13-day ceiling. Its median residency (17 days) exceeds the rule’s maximum.

F2 — The reload cadence

Off-list gaps between residencies are bimodal: six gaps of 7–27 trading days (the “reload” cluster — the fastest possible re-qualification is ~6–7 days, so a 7-day gap means failing resumed immediately after exit), against genuine dormant periods of 61–271 days. The reload cadence is the closest thing to a predictable settlement rhythm in this data.

The fails did not get delivered in the week retail couldn’t buy — the location of the failure moved from the stock to the wrapper. That is what “the wrapper changed” means, precisely.

F4 — The inverted cliff (the surprise)

The naïve expectation: fails build during a residency and drop after the forced close-out. The data shows the opposite in 9 of 12 completed ≥13-day residencies. Mean daily fails over the last three listed days are typically 2,000–10,000 shares — then jump within five days of exit: ×24, ×29, ×54, up to ×85 (the 2025-02→04 residency: 3,886 → 329,813/day). Exit requires five consecutive clean days, and the tails show fails being managed down to satisfy exactly that test, with failing resuming the moment the constraint releases. This is clock management made visible: deliveries are timed to the rule’s measurement windows.

F5 — No standing T+35 wave

Correlating fail quantities against themselves (and GME→XRT) at every calendar lag from 25 to 45 days produced nothing distinguishable at 35. Fixed-lag periodicity is not how this system breathes; the structure is conditional on events — fail clusters, list entries, exits. We publish this null deliberately: it is the rebuttal a referee would reach for first.

III“So did XRT become an instrument of blatant manipulation after Jan 29, 2021?”

Not after — the data says XRT was already the conduit long before. Its first observed residency starts on the first day of our data (Oct 1, 2019) already in progress, and runs 187 days through June 2020. What January 29, 2021 marks is not the beginning of the mechanism but the moment it became visible at maximum size under maximum stress — the one week when the pressure had nowhere else to go, so the handoff happened in the open, dated to the day.

And be precise about what the data can and cannot say, because this is where the paper wins or dies:

  • What it shows: fails relocated from GME to XRT at the squeeze peak; XRT persistently resident on a list designed to be escaped in 13 days; fails suppressed to satisfy exit tests and resumed immediately after. These are measurements.
  • What it cannot show: intent, identity of the failing participants, or illegality. “Manipulation” is a legal conclusion; CNS data is anonymous and aggregate.
  • The sharper claim available: none of this needs to be illegal — that’s the indictment. Threshold close-out obligates purchase of the ETF, not the basket; an authorized participant can satisfy it by creating new XRT shares against a basket that substitutes cash in lieu for the hard-to-borrow component. The clock resets and no GME share was ever bought. To your phrasing — “they press a button and choose cash or the security” — yes: at the creation margin, an XRT share’s GME slice can be an IOU denominated in cash, rolled for as long as the cycle keeps working. The paper’s strongest form: the bona-fide market-making exemption plus cash-in-lieu creation makes Rule 203(b)(3) unenforceable against precisely the instruments it was written for — and the threshold list itself is six years of evidence.

The May 2024 cluster — the largest GME fail days of the year — sits directly in front of the May 13–14 run, with the June run landing ~T+30–35 behind it.
  • Scale: the 2024 peak day (525K) is one-quarter of January 2021’s (2.10M).
  • The denominator moved: the 0.5%-of-outstanding trigger scaled with GME’s share count — roughly 70M shares in January 2021 versus ~350M+ by mid-2024 (post-split, post-offerings; approximate public figures). In 2021 the list trigger sat near 350K fails/day sustained for five days; by 2024 it needed ~1.7M+/day for five straight days. January 2021 ran at 3–6× its trigger; May 2024 peaked at ~0.3× of its own. Dilution didn’t just fund the company — it mechanically retired GME’s ability to ever appear on the threshold list again.
  • XRT stayed quiet in June 2024 (no 10K+ fail day between May 14 and July 15). Whatever carried the June pressure, it wasn’t XRT creations failing — options (the gamma channel), swaps, or other wrappers we haven’t joined yet. The 2021 playbook is one instrument in a set that has since widened.

VToward a predictive model — and the Bitcoin-cycle question

On “Bitcoin has a 1,065-day bull / 365-day bear clock, what if GME is due”: I’d steer the model away from that framing, respectfully but firmly. Fixed-length cycles are pattern without plumbing — with one hypothesis per cycle-length you will always find one that fits the past and none that binds the future. What this dataset offers instead is rarer and better: clocks with mechanisms and statutory dates. Every window below exists because a rule makes someone act by a deadline:

The F5 null tells you the model must be event-conditioned, not periodic: nothing echoes every 35 days, but given a fail cluster, obligations dated ~T+35 out genuinely exist (May 2024 → June 2024 is consistent with exactly that). So the architecture I’d build:

  1. State variables: GME and basket-ETF daily fails; distance-to-trigger (fails ÷ 0.5%-of-outstanding — this normalizes 2021 vs 2024 properly); XRT residency state (on-list, day count, days since exit); fail-cluster age (days since last >N-share cluster).
  2. Event windows: for each detected fail cluster, project the T+35 date and the first OPEX after it; treat those as elevated-probability windows rather than predictions.
  3. The F4 signature as a leading indicator: fails collapsing to near-zero while XRT is still listed = an exit (and therefore a reload window) is being engineered — that’s observable ~5 days before it completes.
  4. Cross-sectional confirmation: extend the join to the rest of the synthetic basket (CHWY, IWM, retail-sector ETFs — your tracker’s README already names them). A GME window that also lights up the basket is a different animal from one that doesn’t.
  5. Data still needed: daily price/volume, GME shares-outstanding history, XRT creation-unit flows and shares outstanding, short interest, options OI by expiry. The first two are the priority: distance-to-trigger is the single most explanatory normalization this analysis surfaced.

Is a “big settlement” due? The honest version: the plumbing guarantees windows, not outcomes — pressure can always be rolled into the next wrapper, which is the core finding of six years of XRT data. What a model can legitimately do is date the windows and measure how much has to roll through them. As of the data’s edge (June 12), XRT is 41+ days into a live residency with 100K–600K fail days and one 3.06M print on May 15 — the machine is running right now, whatever the calendar says.

VICaveats, for the referee in all of us

  • FTD quantities end 2026-06-12; the SEC has not published May 1–15, 2026 (cnsfails202605a) — the downloader will pick it up automatically if it appears.
  • Threshold lists run through 2026-07-02; CBOE’s 2026-07-02 file was still unpublished (403) at write-time and retries automatically.
  • The 187-day residency is left-censored (data starts 2019-10-01 mid-streak); the true figure is ≥187. NYSE’s API no longer serves 2025-10-13 / 2025-11-11; per-exchange calendars make this harmless for streaks.
  • CNS data reports aggregate net fails at NSCC — it undercounts economic failure (ex-clearing, obligation warehouses don’t appear) and cannot attribute fails to any participant.
  • All streak statistics depend on the per-exchange calendar correction (Part I). Replications using naïve weekday or federal-holiday calendars will understate every streak.
